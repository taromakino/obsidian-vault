# Notation

Let $x$ denote a candidate program or idea and $S(x)$ its evaluator score (higher is better). At iteration $t$, $\mathcal{H}_t$ is the full history of evaluated programs, $p_t$ is the selected parent, and $x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,N}$ are its proposed children. The baseline generates $N$ children per iteration for $T$ iterations, giving a total rollout budget $B=NT$.

The **top-$K$ elite archive**, $\mathcal{E}_t^K=\operatorname{TopK}(\mathcal{H}_t,K;S)$, contains the $K$ highest-scoring programs in the history. Here $\mathcal{E}$ is a calligraphic E, not epsilon. The notation $x\sim\mathcal{A}$ means uniform sampling from a finite archive $\mathcal{A}$.

$K$ controls elite archive size, whereas $N$ controls the number of children per iteration. The exploration probability $\epsilon$ is the chance of sampling a parent from the full history instead of the elite archive. For tree search, $\mathcal{D}_t(x)$ is the descendant set of $x$, $Q_t(x)$ is its subtree value, $N_x(t)$ counts expansions of $x$, and $N_p(t)$ is the visit count of its parent. The constants $c_{\mathrm{uct}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{puct}}$ set exploration strength. The history size is $n_t=|\mathcal{H}_t|$, the zero-based evaluator-score rank is $r_t(x)$, and $P_t(x)$ is a rank-based prior.

# Sequential Best-of-N baseline

Sequential BoN starts from an evaluated initial program. At each iteration it selects the highest-scoring program in the entire history, $p_t=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{H}_t}S(x)$, proposes $N$ modifications, evaluates them, and appends them to the history. It repeats for $T$ iterations, using $B=NT$ rollouts.

The selected parent can come from any earlier iteration, not necessarily the most recent batch. If none of the new children improves the best score, the next iteration can expand the same parent again. BoN is therefore greedy and elitist: it exploits the best known program without explicitly encouraging alternative lineages.

# From BoN to OpenEvolve

OpenEvolve-style search relaxes the greedy policy in stages. **First, a top-$K$ elite archive** replaces the top-1 choice: $p_t\sim\mathcal{E}_t^K$. One parent is sampled uniformly from the $K$ best programs, allowing multiple high-scoring lineages to be revisited. $K=1$ recovers BoN.

**Second, epsilon-greedy exploration** occasionally samples a parent uniformly from the entire evaluated history, including non-elite programs. This can recover neglected lineages, but it also spends evaluations on lower-scoring parents.

$$
p_t\sim\begin{cases}
\mathcal{E}_t^K,&\text{with probability }1-\epsilon,\\
\mathcal{H}_t,&\text{with probability }\epsilon.
\end{cases}
$$

**Third, the breadth–depth allocation changes.** With $B=NT$ fixed, reducing the number of children $N$ per expansion allows more sequential iterations $T$. This gives the search more opportunities for successive refinement rather than generating many siblings from one parent.

**Finally, OpenEvolve adds context and parallelism.** Inspiration sampling provides the model with additional historical programs besides the selected parent. MAP-Elites helps maintain diverse inspirations. Multi-island evolution maintains several search histories in parallel and periodically exchanges strong programs. These additions change the context and population structure without changing the basic generate–evaluate–archive loop.

# From BoN to TTT-Discover-style search

TTT-Discover-style search represents program modifications as a tree. Each node is an evaluated program, and an edge connects a parent to a child generated by modifying it. Its central change is to select parents by the promise of further exploration, rather than by individual evaluator score alone.

**Subtree value.** A node receives credit for the best score achieved by itself or any descendant: $Q_t(x)=\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}_t(x)\cup\{x\}}S(d)$. An intermediate program can therefore be a useful starting point even when its own score is not the highest.

**UCT exploration.** Upper Confidence Bounds applied to Trees combines this subtree value with an exploration bonus. The bonus declines when a node has been expanded repeatedly, so promising but less-explored branches can receive attention.

$$
\operatorname{UCT}_t(x)=Q_t(x)+c_{\mathrm{uct}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(N_p(t)+1)}{1+N_x(t)}}
$$

**PUCT prior.** PUCT weights the exploration bonus by $P_t(x)$, a prior that favors programs with higher individual evaluator-score ranks. Subtree value reflects what a branch has produced, while the prior reflects the node's own quality. The expansion count reduces the bonus for nodes already explored extensively.

$$
\operatorname{PUCT}_t(x)=Q_t(x)+c_{\mathrm{puct}}P_t(x)\frac{\sqrt{1+t}}{1+N_x(t)}
$$

The rank-based prior is $P_t(x)=\frac{n_t-r_t(x)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_t}i}$. Parent selection is $p_t=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{H}_t}\operatorname{PUCT}_t(x)$. The prior is **not** used directly to sample a parent: it weights the exploration bonus, and the algorithm selects the highest PUCT score. A larger prior does not guarantee selection. The studied TTT-Discover-style variant can also expand multiple parents in one iteration.

# Statistical evaluation in Section 3

The paper evaluates each harness on 12 model–problem pairs: four models and three problems. For each pair, the target harness is run five independent times. BoN has a larger reference pool—100 runs for each Qwen pair and 30 for each GPT-OSS pair—so that its run-to-run variation can be estimated. Comparisons use matched rollout budgets.

## Pair-level p-value

For one particular model–problem pair:

1. Run the target harness five times and take the maximum score, $T_h$.

2. Sample five completed BoN runs **with replacement** from the BoN reference pool and take their maximum, $T_r^0$.

3. Repeat the BoN sampling 100,000 times, keeping the target harness's observed maximum fixed.

4. Compute the fraction of bootstrap samples in which BoN matches or exceeds the target harness.

This fraction, with a small finite-sample correction, is the pair-level p-value $\hat p_h$. A small value means that the target harness's observed best-of-five result is unusual relative to the BoN reference distribution. It is not the probability that the target harness is better.

## Aggregate majority-win probability

For all 12 model–problem pairs:

1. Run the target harness five times for each pair and take the maximum within each pair. This produces a vector of 12 observed target-harness scores.

2. Independently sample five BoN runs with replacement for each pair and take the maximum within each pair. This produces a comparable vector of 12 BoN scores.

3. Compare the two vectors pair by pair and record whether the **target harness wins at least 7 of the 12 pairs**. A tie does not count as a target-harness win.

4. Repeat the BoN bootstrap 100,000 times and calculate the fraction of comparisons in which the target harness wins a majority.

This fraction is the aggregate **majority-win probability**, $\hat P_{\mathrm{maj}}(h)$, **not a p-value**. It measures how often the target harness's *fixed observed* best-of-five results beat a resampled BoN best-of-five benchmark on a majority of pairs.

## Aggregate p-value and multiple comparisons

To test whether this majority-win probability is statistically unusual, the authors perform a separate permutation test:

1. Within each model–problem pair, pool the five target-harness scores with the larger BoN reference pool.

2. Randomly assign five pooled scores to a hypothetical target harness and the remaining scores to a hypothetical BoN baseline.

3. Recalculate the aggregate majority-win probability for that reassignment, using the same BoN resampling procedure.

4. Repeat 100,000 times. The fraction of permutations whose majority-win probability is at least the observed $\hat P_{\mathrm{maj}}(h)$, with a finite-sample correction, is the aggregate p-value $\hat p_{\mathrm{cross}}(h)$.

The permutation test asks whether the observed aggregate advantage could plausibly arise if the target harness and BoN had the same score distributions. Because many harness configurations are tested, the authors also apply **Holm's correction** to the aggregate p-values. An uncorrected p-value below 0.05 may cease to be significant after this adjustment.

In short, $\hat p_h$ tests one model–problem pair, $\hat P_{\mathrm{maj}}(h)$ measures the observed breadth of aggregate advantage against bootstrapped BoN, and $\hat p_{\mathrm{cross}}(h)$ tests whether that aggregate statistic is significant. These Section 3 metrics differ from the mean-score evaluation of adaptive budget allocation in Section 5.

# Main results

**No fixed harness is a statistically established aggregate winner.** Across 30 budget-matched configurations and 12 model–problem pairs, the best observed fixed configuration is epsilon-greedy selection with $K=1$ and $\epsilon=20\%$. Its majority-win probability against resampled BoN is $0.914$, with an uncorrected aggregate p-value of $0.023$. After Holm correction, the p-value is $0.678$, so the result is not statistically significant under the paper's multiple-comparison criterion. This does not imply the harness has no effect; it means the experiment does not establish a reliable aggregate improvement.

**More complex OpenEvolve components do not yield monotonic gains.** Larger top-$K$ archives alone do not achieve significant pair-level improvements in the reported best-of-five tests. Epsilon-greedy exploration helps some model–problem pairs and hurts others. Shifting compute from breadth to depth, adding MAP-Elites inspirations, and using multiple islands do not consistently improve the outcome. Full OpenEvolve-style configurations often perform worse than simpler intermediate variants.

**Tree-based parent selection is a promising component.** The largest observed gains in the TTT-Discover-style progression tend to appear after introducing UCT or PUCT selection. Additional depth and multiple-parent expansion do not consistently improve those gains. The benefit depends on the model and task, and these fixed tree-search configurations do not establish a significant aggregate advantage after correction.

**Early search progress predicts later outcomes.** At the halfway checkpoint, the Spearman correlation between partial-run and final performance exceeds $0.70$ for 11 of the 12 model–problem pairs (and is $0.651$ for the remaining pair). This motivates reallocating compute while a discovery run is in progress.

**Adaptive allocation improves average performance in the evaluated setting.** For each model–problem pair, the policy samples an initial set of 12 distinct search-harness configurations from the evaluated harness pool. The 12 runs are not the 12 model–problem pairs, and the authors do not select them based on the aggregate harness rankings from Section 4. Instead, they test whether performance observed early on the current problem is useful for choosing where to spend the remaining compute. The strongest policy prunes the 12 partial runs to 5, then 2, then 1 at the 25%, 50%, and 75% checkpoints, under a budget equivalent to five complete runs. Its average final score across the 12 pairs is $85.75\%$, compared with $84.35\%$ for five complete BoN runs and $84.54\%$ for a non-adaptive ensemble of different harnesses. These scores come from empirical resampling of recorded runs, rather than new live executions of the adaptive policy.

An informed initial portfolio—for example, one chosen using prior aggregate results while preserving diversity—might perform differently, but the paper does not test this. Its adaptive-allocation result therefore supports online pruning from a sampled portfolio, not the claim that the initial 12 configurations were optimally chosen.

Overall, harness choice behaves like a model- and problem-dependent hyperparameter. The clearest practical direction in this study is to use partial-run feedback to allocate compute, not to assume that a single more elaborate search recipe will dominate.
